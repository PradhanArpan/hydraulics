1.1 Introduction to Computational Fluid Dynamics
1.1.1 Definition
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is a field of fluid mechanics that utilizes numerical
methods and algorithms to analyze fluid flow problems. It involves solving the fundamental
governing equations of fluid motion, including the Navier-Stokes equations, continuity
equations, and energy equations, using computational techniques. CFD is widely used
across various industries, including aerospace, automotive, civil engineering, and biomedical
applications. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is a specialized discipline within
fluid mechanics that employs numerical methods and algorithms to solve and analyze
problems involving fluid flows. It enables engineers and scientists to simulate complex fluid
dynamics phenomena within a computational framework, reducing reliance on costly and
time consuming experimentation.
CFD integrates fundamental physics, mathematics, and computational sciences to
provide detailed insights into fluid behavior. The governing equations of CFD, primarily
the Navier-Stokes equations, describe the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy in
a fluid flow system. These equations are solved using advanced numerical techniques and
computational power.
1.1.2 Scope of CFD
CFD has extensive applications across multiple engineering and scientific fields, including:

1.2 Simplified forms of the Navier-Stokes equations
The Navier-Stokes (NS) equations describe the motion of fluid flow, governing conservation
of mass, momentum, and energy. However, their full form is highly complex and often
requires simplifications for practical Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations.
Two common simplified forms are the Euler equations and the Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes
(RANS) equations.
1.2.1 Euler Equations
Assumptions
➡ Inviscid flow: Neglects viscosity (µ = 0), meaning no shear stresses.
➡ Irrotational flow: Assumes no vorticity (though not always required).
➡ No turbulence modeling: Does not account for small-scale fluctuations.
Derivation
Starting from the Navier-Stokes equations, the momentum equation is:
∂u
∂t + (u · ∇)u = − 1
ρ ∇p + ν∇2u + g (1.1)
Setting viscosity ν = 0 (inviscid assumption) removes the viscous term (ν∇2u), leading
to:
∂u
∂t + (u · ∇)u = − 1
ρ ∇p + g (1.2)
Additionally, the continuity equation remains:
∂ ρ
∂t + ∇ · (ρu) =0 (1.3)
Application in CFD
➡ Used for high-speed aerodynamics and compressible flows where viscous effects are
negligible.
➡ Often applied in external flows, such as supersonic/hypersonic aircraft design.
➡ Less accurate for boundary layers and turbulent regions due to the neglect of viscosity.
1.2.2 Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) Equations
Assumptions
➡ Turbulent flow decomposition: Velocity is decomposed into mean and fluctuating
components:
u = u + u′ (1.4)
where u is the time-averaged velocity and u′ is the fluctuating part.

➡ Reynolds averaging: Time-averaging removes small turbulent fluctuations.
➡ Closure models required: Since turbulence introduces unknowns, additional models
(like k-ε or k-ω) are needed.
Derivation
Reynolds averaging the Navier-Stokes momentum equation gives:
∂u
∂t + (u · ∇)u = − 1
ρ ∇p + ν∇2u − ∇ · u′u′ (1.5)
where the term −∇ · u′u′ represents the Reynolds stress tensor, which must be modeled
using turbulence models.
Application in CFD
➡ Widely used in engineering applications like aircraft design, HV AC systems, and
environmental flows.
➡ Provides an efficient way to model turbulent flows without resolving every small
fluctuation (as in Direct Numerical Simulation, DNS).
➡ Used in steady-state and unsteady CFD simulations, but requires careful selection of
turbulence models (e.g., k-ε, k-ω).
1.2.3 Conclusion
➡ Euler equations are useful for high-speed, inviscid flows but cannot capture turbulence
or boundary layer effects.
➡ RANS equations simplify turbulent flows using averaging, making them ideal for
industrial and engineering applications.
➡ The choice depends on the problem: Euler for inviscid high-speed flow, RANS for
practical turbulent flow modeling.

1.3 Numerical Methods for CFD
Numerical discretization is fundamental in Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) as it
enables the transformation of governing differential equations into algebraic equations that
can be solved using computational methods. Several numerical techniques exist, each with
distinct advantages and applications. The most commonly used methods in CFD include:
1.3.1 Finite Difference Method (FDM)
The Finite Difference Method (FDM) is based on approximating the derivatives of the
governing partial differential equations (PDEs) using numerical differentiation. The domain
is discretized into a structured grid, and derivatives are estimated using difference equations
such as forward, backward, or central differences.
Advantages:
1. Simple to implement, especially for structured grids.
2. Computationally efficient for problems with regular geometries.
Disadvantages:
1. Less flexible for complex geometries. Accuracy depends on the choice of grid spacing
and numerical scheme (explicit vs. implicit).
2. FDM is widely used in problems involving Cartesian grids and for solving problems
such as boundary layer flows and turbulence modeling.
1.3.2 Finite Volume Method (FVM)
The Finite V olume Method (FVM) discretizes the governing equations in their integral form,
ensuring conservation of mass, momentum, and energy over control volumes. The domain
is divided into small control volumes (cells), and fluxes across the cell faces are evaluated
using interpolation and numerical integration techniques.
Advantages:
1. Strict conservation properties, making it suitable for fluid flow problems.
2. Flexibility in handling unstructured grids and complex geometries.
Disadvantages:
1. More computationally intensive compared to FDM.
2. Requires careful selection of interpolation and flux reconstruction schemes.
3. FVM is extensively used in commercial CFD solvers such as ANSYS Fluent and
OpenFOAM due to its robustness in solving Navier-Stokes equations.
1.3.3 Finite Element Method (FEM)
The Finite Element Method (FEM) employs variational principles and weighted residual
methods to approximate solutions. It is particularly useful for solving problems involving
complex geometries and irregular boundaries. The computational domain is divided into

--- Page 12 ---
12 Chapter 1. Computational Fluid Dynamics
elements, and shape functions (basis functions) are used to approximate the solution within
each element.
Advantages:
1. Excellent for handling complex geometries.
2. Can accommodate higher-order accuracy through polynomial basis functions.
Disadvantages:
1. Computationally expensive due to matrix assembly and inversion.
2. More complex implementation compared to FDM and FVM.
FEM is commonly used in structural mechanics and multiphysics simulations but is also
applied in CFD, particularly for problems involving porous media and fluid-structure
interactions.
Each of these methods has its strengths and limitations, and the choice of method
depends on the specific nature of the CFD problem, the desired accuracy, and computational
efficiency.

1.4 Turbulence Modeling in Fluid Dynamics
Turbulence is a complex, chaotic state of fluid motion characterized by vortices, eddies,
and fluctuating velocity fields. It occurs in most real-world flows, including air around an
aircraft, water in rivers, and wind in urban environments.
Solving the Navier-Stokes equations directly for turbulent flows requires resolving all
scales of motion, which is computationally infeasible for most practical problems. This is
why turbulence modeling is necessary—to approximate turbulent effects while keeping
computations manageable.
1.4.1 Nature of Turbulence: Unsteady, Chaotic Motion
Turbulence is a complex phenomenon in fluid dynamics characterized by irregular, three-dimensional
vortices and rapid variations in velocity and pressure. It occurs when the inertial forces in a
fluid flow dominate over viscous forces, leading to an unpredictable and chaotic motion of
fluid particles.
Turbulence commonly occurs in high Reynolds number flows, where:
➡ There is a significant difference in velocity between fluid layers.
➡ Flow interactions create swirling eddies of different scales.
➡ Energy cascades from large vortices to smaller ones until it dissipates as heat.
Examples of turbulence in real-world applications include:
➡ Airflow over an aircraft wing:
The boundary layer transitions from laminar to turbulent, affecting lift and drag forces.
➡ River flows:
Rapid and turbulent currents occur around obstacles like boulders, bridge piers, and
bends.
➡ Combustion in engines:
Turbulent mixing of fuel and air enhances combustion efficiency.
➡ Atmospheric turbulence:
Wind patterns create turbulence in weather systems, affecting cloud formation and
dispersion of pollutants.
➡ Turbulent mixing in oceans:
Plays a crucial role in nutrient distribution and energy transfer.
1.4.2 Importance of Turbulence Modeling
Turbulence modeling is essential for accurate simulations in engineering and environmental
studies due to the following reasons:
➡ Predicting Flow Behavior:
Understanding how turbulence affects velocity profiles, pressure distribution, and
shear stresses is critical for design and performance evaluation.
➡ Enhancing Simulation Accuracy:
Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) of turbulence is computationally expensive,
making turbulence models necessary for practical applications.

➡ Designing Efficient Systems:
Engineering applications, such as aircraft aerodynamics, hydraulic structures, and
HV AC systems, require turbulence modeling to optimize performance.
➡ Safety and Reliability:
Accurate turbulence prediction helps mitigate risks associated with extreme weather
conditions, fluid-structure interactions, and industrial processes.
Turbulence modeling remains a fundamental aspect of computational fluid dynamics (CFD),
enabling engineers and scientists to analyze and optimize fluid flow behavior in complex
environments.
1.4.3 Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) Models
k-ε Model
➡ Solves two transport equations (turbulent kinetic energy k and dissipation rate ε).
➡ Pros: Robust, widely used for industrial flows.
➡ Cons: Struggles with flow separation and near-wall accuracy.
➡ Application: HV AC, pipelines, hydrodynamics, civil structures.
k-ω Model
➡ Uses ω (specific dissipation rate) instead of ε.
➡ Pros: Better for boundary layers and low-Re flows.
➡ Cons: More sensitive to freestream turbulence conditions.
➡ Application: Aerospace, marine hydrodynamics, wind tunnels.
Shear Stress Transport (SST) Model
➡ Hybrid of k-ε and k-ω uses k-ω near walls and k-ε in free-stream regions.
➡ Pros: Improved accuracy for separation and adverse pressure gradients.
➡ Cons: More computationally expensive than standard RANS.
➡ Application: Airfoil aerodynamics, ship hulls, turbomachinery.
1.4.4 Large Eddy Simulation (LES)
➡ Resolves large turbulence structures while modeling small-scale effects using a
sub-grid scale (SGS) model.
➡ Pros: Captures unsteady turbulence more accurately than RANS.
➡ Cons: Requires fine meshes and high computational power.
➡ Application: Jet engines, combustion modeling, meteorology, aerospace.
1.4.5 Detached Eddy Simulation (DES)
➡ Combines LES for large turbulent structures and RANS near walls.
➡ Pros: Reduces computational cost compared to full LES.
➡ Cons: Less accurate than pure LES for some flows.
➡ Application: Car aerodynamics, high-Re flows, bluff body flows.

1.5 Comparison of Grids in CFD Simulations
In Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD), grid generation is crucial for accurate simulations.
While OpenFOAM primarily uses structured, unstructured, and hybrid grids for CFD
applications, HEC-RAS also employs different grid types for hydraulic modeling, particularly
in its 2D and unsteady flow simulations.
1.5.1 Structured Grid
Description:
Consists of regular, evenly spaced grids (typically quadrilateral or hexahedral cells).
Advantages:
➡ More accurate and computationally efficient due to alignment with flow direction.
➡ Requires less memory and processing power.
➡ Easier to apply numerical schemes with reduced numerical diffusion.
Disadvantages:
➡ Difficult to use for complex geometries like river meanders or floodplains.
➡ Requires significant pre-processing to fit irregular boundaries.
Use in HEC-RAS:
➡ Typically not used for 1D simulations.
➡ 2D flow areas in HEC-RAS can have structured grids, though it is less common.
1.5.2 Unstructured Grid
Description:
Composed of irregularly shaped elements (triangular or tetrahedral in 2D/3D).
Advantages:
➡ Suitable for complex geometries, such as rivers with varying cross-sections and
floodplain expansions.
➡ Can adapt to changes in resolution, refining the grid where necessary.
Disadvantages:
➡ Higher computational cost due to irregular connectivity of nodes.
➡ Can introduce numerical diffusion if not properly refined.
Use in HEC-RAS:

1.6 Experimental Validation and Calibration in CFD
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations provide a numerical approach to solving
fluid flow problems, but their reliability depends on proper validation and calibration.
Experimental validation ensures that the numerical model accurately represents real-world
physics, while calibration fine-tunes the model parameters for improved accuracy. Without
experimental validation, CFD predictions may be uncertain and lack credibility in engineering
applications.
1.6.1 Importance of Experimental Data in CFD
Experimental data plays a crucial role in CFD simulations by providing a benchmark against
which numerical results can be compared. This validation process helps ensure that the
numerical methods, boundary conditions, and turbulence models accurately capture the
physics of fluid flow.
1. Need for validation using experimental results.
2. Sources of experimental data (lab-scale and field-scale measurements).
1.6.2 Need for Validation Using Experimental Results
Validation is essential for:
➡ Assessing Accuracy:
Comparing simulation results with experimental data helps quantify numerical errors
and deviations.
➡ Ensuring Model Reliability:
Establishing confidence in the CFD model before it is used for design, optimization,
or decision-making.
➡ Identifying Limitations:
Highlighting areas where improvements in grid resolution, turbulence modeling, or
boundary conditions are required.
➡ Regulatory Compliance:
Many industries, such as aerospace, automotive, and environmental engineering,
require CFD results to be validated against experimental findings before approval.
In cases where significant discrepancies exist between CFD and experiments, model
calibration is performed by adjusting parameters such as turbulence closure coefficients,
wall roughness, or boundary conditions.
1.6.3 Sources of Experimental Data
Experimental data for CFD validation can be obtained from controlled laboratory-scale
experiments or large-scale field measurements. Each source has its advantages and challenges.
➡ A. Lab-Scale Measurements
Laboratory experiments are conducted under controlled conditions, allowing for
high-precision data collection. Common setups include:

1. Wind Tunnels:
Used in aerospace and automotive applications to measure aerodynamics and
turbulence characteristics.
2. Water Flumes & Hydraulic Channels:

hydrodynamics.
3. Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV):

Advantages:

2. Controlled boundary conditions enable systematic studies.
Challenges:

2. Limited capability for replicating large-scale or highly complex systems.
➡ B. Field-Scale Measurements
Field experiments provide real-world data under natural conditions, making them
highly valuable for validation. Common sources include:
1. Environmental Monitoring:
Air and water quality measurements, pollutant dispersion studies, and meteorological
observations.
2. Full-Scale Wind Tunnel or Open-Field Tests:
Used in the assessment of building aerodynamics, wind energy studies, and
urban air quality modeling.
3. Flow Measurements in Rivers and Coastal Areas:
Acoustic Doppler Current Profilers (ADCP) and remote sensing techniques help
capture large-scale flow patterns.
Advantages:
1. Realistic representation of operational conditions.
2. Suitable for complex and large-scale applications where lab replication is not
feasible.
Challenges:
1. Difficult to control environmental variables (temperature, pressure, humidity,
etc.).
2. Expensive and time-consuming compared to lab-scale experiments.
Both lab-scale and field-scale measurements contribute significantly to improving CFD
simulations.

HEC-RAS enables engineers and hydrologists to analyze steady and unsteady flow conditions,
perform sediment transport simulations, and evaluate water quality impacts.
1.7.2 Key Features of HEC-RAS
HEC-RAS includes a range of features that make it suitable for hydraulic modeling:
➡ Steady and Unsteady Flow Analysis:
Supports one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) flow modeling to simulate
various hydraulic conditions.
➡ Sediment Transport Modeling:
Predicts sediment erosion, transport, and deposition over time, which is essential for
river and reservoir management.
➡ Water Quality Analysis:
Assesses pollutant dispersion and water quality parameters such as temperature,
dissolved oxygen, and nutrient concentrations.
➡ Bridge and Culvert Analysis:
Evaluates hydraulic impacts of bridges, culverts, and other structures on river flow,
aiding in flood risk assessment.
➡ Floodplain Mapping:
Generates flood inundation maps that help in flood risk management and urban
planning.
➡ Dam Breach Analysis:
Simulates dam failure scenarios to assess potential downstream impacts and mitigation
strategies.
➡ Geospatial Integration:
Works with GIS software for improved spatial analysis and visualization of hydraulic
simulations.

1.8 Uniform and Non-Uniform Flow in Open Channel Simulations
1.8.1 Uniform Flow:
➡ In uniform flow, the depth and velocity of water remain constant along the length of
the channel.
➡ It typically occurs in long, prismatic channels where the slope, roughness, and
cross-section remain unchanged.
➡ The driving force (gravity) and resisting force (friction) balance each other, resulting
in steady flow conditions.
➡ A key example is normal flow in a straight river reach, which can be estimated using
Manning’s equation.
1.8.2 Non-Uniform Flow:
➡ Non-uniform flow occurs when water depth and velocity change along the channel.
➡ It can be classified into gradually varied flow (GVF) and rapidly varied flow (RVF).
➡ GVF occurs when there are gradual changes in flow depth due to backwater effects,
obstructions, or mild channel slopes.
➡ RVF happens in cases of sudden changes, such as at hydraulic jumps, weirs, or
spillways.
1.8.3 Using HEC-RAS for Uniform and Non-Uniform Flow Simulation
HEC-RAS is widely used for open-channel flow simulations, including both uniform and
non-uniform flow scenarios.
Uniform Flow in HEC-RAS:
➡ Can be approximated by ensuring a long, prismatic channel with a steady flow regime.
➡ Manning’s equation is used for normal depth calculations.
➡ The steady-flow analysis module in HEC-RAS can compute flow profiles under
uniform conditions.
Non-Uniform Flow in HEC-RAS:
➡ The software’s steady and unsteady flow analysis tools help model gradually and
rapidly varied flows.
➡ Backwater curves, hydraulic jumps, and flow transitions can be simulated using
energy and momentum equations.
➡ The unsteady flow module allows dynamic analysis of flood waves, dam breaks, and
tidal effects.

Scenario: A river reach with a mild slope and a downstream dam.

➡ Define a long, straight river reach with a constant bed slope and cross-section.
