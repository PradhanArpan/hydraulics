## Same water, two possible depths

A wide canal carries **1.5 m³/s for every metre of its width**. The water can be deep and slow, or shallow and fast.

```predict
q: Squash the same flow into a shallower depth. What happens to the speed of the water?
o: It gets faster
o: It gets slower
o: It stays the same
a: 1
why: The same amount of water must still get through. A smaller opening means the water has to move faster. In symbols, $q = V\,y$.
```

Today you will find out which of the two depths a channel actually "chooses", and why one special depth in the middle matters so much.

## Three words to remember

**Specific energy $E$** is the energy of the water, measured as a height in metres, taken from the channel bed. It has two parts: the depth, and the "speed" part.

$$E = y + \frac{V^2}{2g}$$

*In words: energy = depth + speed energy.*

**Critical depth $y_c$** is the one depth where $E$ is smallest for a given flow. Any other depth needs more energy.

**Froude number $Fr$** compares the water's speed with the speed of a ripple on its surface.

$$Fr = \frac{V}{\sqrt{g\,y}}$$

*In words: is the current faster or slower than a ripple?*

| Fr | Name | What it looks like |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| below 1 | **Subcritical** | Deep and calm. Ripples can go upstream. |
| equal to 1 | **Critical** | The lowest-energy state. Ripples stand still. |
| above 1 | **Supercritical** | Shallow and fast. Ripples are swept downstream. |

> [!note] Why this matters
> If ripples can go upstream, a gate or a weir downstream can "tell" the water behind it. That is why subcritical flow is controlled from the **downstream** end, and supercritical flow from the **upstream** end. You will use this again in Day 16.

## Where does the critical depth come from?

Click through one step at a time. Each step has a reason.

```steps
say: Write the energy using only depth and flow per metre width. We replace $V$ by $q/y$.
eq: E = y + \frac{q^2}{2g\,y^2}
why: The flow $q$ is fixed, so depth is the only thing that can change.
---
say: The lowest point of a curve is where its slope is zero. Differentiate with respect to $y$.
eq: \frac{dE}{dy} = 1 - \frac{q^2}{g\,y^3}
why: This is the slope of the curve you saw in the diagram.
---
say: Set the slope to zero and solve for the depth. That depth is the critical depth.
eq: y_c = \left(\frac{q^2}{g}\right)^{1/3}
why: Zero slope means the bottom of the curve.
---
say: At this depth, $q = V\,y$ gives $V^2/(g\,y_c) = 1$. So the Froude number is exactly 1.
eq: Fr = 1
why: This is why critical flow and Fr = 1 are the same thing.
---
say: Put $q^2 = g\,y_c^3$ back into $E$ to find the lowest possible energy.
eq: E_c = y_c + \frac{y_c}{2} = 1.5\,y_c
why: At critical flow the speed energy is exactly half the depth.
```

## Find the critical depth and the other depth

> [!note] Problem
> A rectangular channel is 4 m wide and carries $Q = 12\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$. The water is 2.0 m deep. Find the critical depth, the specific energy, and the other depth that carries the same flow with the same energy.

**Step 1. Flow per metre width.** $q = Q/b = 12/4 = 3\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$.

**Step 2. Critical depth.** $y_c = (q^2/g)^{1/3} = (9/9.81)^{1/3} = 0.972$ m. The lowest possible energy is $E_c = 1.5 \times 0.972 = 1.458$ m.

**Step 3. Where are we?** $V = q/y = 3/2.0 = 1.50$ m/s and $Fr = 1.5/\sqrt{9.81 \times 2.0} = 0.34$. The flow is **subcritical** (deep and calm), and $y = 2.0$ m is above $y_c$.

**Step 4. The energy.** $E = y + V^2/2g = 2.0 + 1.5^2/19.62 = 2.115$ m. That is more than $E_c$, as it should be.

**Step 5. The other depth.** Solve $y + 0.4587/y^2 = 2.115$ for the value **below** $y_c$. Trial and error gives $y' = 0.54$ m. There the speed is $V' = 3/0.54 = 5.56$ m/s and $Fr = 2.4$, so it is **supercritical**.

> [!note] What to take away
> The same flow can be deep and slow (2.0 m, 1.5 m/s) or shallow and fast (0.54 m, 5.56 m/s) with the same energy. Which one occurs depends on what the channel does downstream and upstream, not on the flow alone.
