## Why two pumps in parallel don't give double the water

A contractor needs to double the water flow rate pumped through a $5$ km pipeline. To save money, they buy an identical second pump and connect it in parallel with the first.

When they turn both pumps on, instead of getting double the water ($200\%$), they get only $115\%$! Why did adding a second pump give almost no extra water?

```predict
q: When water pumps are connected in parallel into a long, high-friction pipeline, why doesn't the discharge double?
o: The pumps fight each other
o: Because pipe friction loss grows with the square of flow (h_f = K * Q^2)! Doubling flow quadruples friction head, causing the pump operating point to move to a much higher head where the pumps deliver far less flow.
o: Parallel connection only works for electricity, not water
a: 2
why: In a pipeline with high friction, doubling discharge requires four times higher head. Parallel pumps add discharge, not head. In a steep system curve, pumps should be connected in series to double head, not parallel!
```

Today you will see how the **pump curve** intersects the **pipeline system curve**, and how **NPSH** prevents cavitation.

## System Curves, Pump Combinations & NPSH

### 1. The Pipeline System Characteristic Curve
A pipeline's total head requirement increases as flow increases due to friction:

$$H_{\text{sys}} = H_{\text{stat}} + K\,Q^2$$

- $H_{\text{stat}} = h_s + h_d$: Constant static elevation difference.
- $K\,Q^2 = \left(\frac{4 f L}{2 g D A^2} + \sum \frac{K_L}{2 g A^2}\right) Q^2$: Friction and minor losses.

The **Operating Point (Duty Point)** occurs at the unique intersection:
$$H_{\text{pump}}(Q) = H_{\text{sys}}(Q)$$

### 2. Pumps in Series vs Pumps in Parallel

| Feature | Pumps in Series | Pumps in Parallel |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **How connected?** | Discharge of Pump 1 enters suction of Pump 2 | Both draw from sump and deliver into common manifold |
| **Head & Discharge** | Total head doubles ($H_{\text{total}} = 2 H$), flow is same | Total discharge doubles ($Q_{\text{total}} = 2 Q$), head is same |
| **Best used when?** | Pipeline has high static lift or long high-friction pipes (steep system curve) | Pipeline has low lift and short wide pipes (flat system curve) |

### 3. Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH)
To prevent cavitation on the impeller inlet eye:

$$\text{NPSH}_a = \frac{p_{\text{atm}} - p_v}{\rho\,g} - h_s - h_{fs}$$

- **$\text{NPSH}_a$ (Available):** Head margin provided by atmospheric pressure above vapor pressure after subtracting suction lift $h_s$ and suction friction $h_{fs}$.
- **$\text{NPSH}_r$ (Required):** Minimum head margin required by the manufacturer's impeller design to prevent cavitation.

$$\text{Cavitation Safety Criterion: } \text{NPSH}_a \ge \text{NPSH}_r + 0.5\ \text{m}$$

*If $\text{NPSH}_a < \text{NPSH}_r$, water flashes into vapor bubbles at the impeller eye, producing loud rattling noise, vibration, and pitting erosion!*

## Derive analytical operating point for pump and system

Click through the algebraic intersection of pump and pipeline curves.

```steps
say: Approximate the pump head-capacity curve as a parabolic drooping curve.
eq: H_{\text{pump}} = H_0 - A\,Q^2
why: H0 is the shut-off head at zero flow, and A represents internal impeller and casing friction losses.
---
say: Write the pipeline system head equation with static lift and pipe friction.
eq: H_{\text{sys}} = H_{\text{stat}} + K\,Q^2
why: Energy equation across the pipeline network.
---
say: At the operating duty point, pump generated head equals system required head.
eq: H_0 - A\,Q^2 = H_{\text{stat}} + K\,Q^2
why: The intersection defines the only point where mass and energy are simultaneously satisfied.
---
say: Group all Q^2 terms on one side.
eq: (A + K)\,Q^2 = H_0 - H_{\text{stat}} \implies Q_{\text{op}} = \sqrt{\frac{H_0 - H_{\text{stat}}}{A + K}}
why: The operating discharge is determined by the ratio of excess shut-off head to total combined resistance!
```

## Sizing a parallel pumping system for water supply

> [!note] Problem
> A municipal booster station lifts water against a static head $H_{\text{stat}} = 15.0$ m through a pipeline with resistance coefficient $K = 500\ \text{m}/(\text{m}^3/\text{s})^2$ ($H_{\text{sys}} = 15 + 500 Q^2$).
> Each identical pump has head-capacity curve:
> $$H_{\text{pump}} = 40 - 300\,Q^2 \quad (\text{with } Q \text{ in } \text{m}^3/\text{s})$$
> Calculate:
> 1. Operating discharge $Q_1$ and head $H_1$ for a single pump
> 2. Operating discharge $Q_2$ and head $H_2$ when two identical pumps operate in parallel
> 3. The percentage increase in discharge achieved by adding the second pump

**Step 1. Operating point for single pump:**
Equate pump head to system head:
$$40 - 300\,Q^2 = 15 + 500\,Q^2$$
$$(300 + 500)\,Q^2 = 40 - 15 \implies 800\,Q^2 = 25$$
$$Q_1^2 = \frac{25}{800} = 0.03125 \implies Q_1 = 0.1768\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} = 176.8\ \text{L}/\text{s}$$
$$H_1 = 15 + 500(0.03125) = 15 + 15.625 = 30.63\ \text{m}$$

**Step 2. Combined curve for two pumps in parallel:**
In parallel, for a given head, total discharge is $Q = 2 q_{\text{each}} \implies q_{\text{each}} = Q/2$.
$$H_{\text{parallel}} = 40 - 300\left(\frac{Q}{2}\right)^2 = 40 - \frac{300}{4} Q^2 = 40 - 75\,Q^2$$
Equate parallel pump curve to system curve:
$$40 - 75\,Q^2 = 15 + 500\,Q^2$$
$$(500 + 75)\,Q^2 = 40 - 15 \implies 575\,Q^2 = 25$$
$$Q_2^2 = \frac{25}{575} = 0.04348 \implies Q_2 = 0.2085\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} = 208.5\ \text{L}/\text{s}$$
$$H_2 = 15 + 500(0.04348) = 15 + 21.74 = 36.74\ \text{m}$$

**Step 3. Percentage increase in discharge:**
$$\% \text{ increase} = \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_1} \times 100\% = \frac{208.5 - 176.8}{176.8} \times 100\% = \frac{31.7}{176.8} \times 100\% = 17.93\%$$

> [!note] What to take away
> Doubling the pumps in parallel did **not** double discharge ($+100\%$); it only yielded a modest **$17.9\%$** boost because pipe friction jumped from $15.6$ m to $21.7$ m! To double discharge, the pipeline diameter must be enlarged.

## Quick check

```quiz
q: When should pumps be installed in series rather than parallel?
o: When available electrical voltage is low
o: When the pipeline is long with high static lift and high friction (steep system curve)
o: When the water is muddy
a: 2
why: Series pumps add their heads together (H_total = H_1 + H_2), providing the high pressure required to overcome steep static lift and heavy pipe friction.
---
q: What must be true to guarantee that a centrifugal pump will NOT cavitate?
o: NPSH Available must be strictly greater than NPSH Required (NPSHa >= NPSHr + margin)
o: The pump must be painted white
o: Motor speed must be zero
a: 1
why: NPSH Available measures actual liquid pressure margin above boiling; it must exceed the manufacturer's required threshold to prevent bubble formation.
```

## Civil engineering connections

- **Lift Irrigation Schemes:** In mega-lift schemes (like the Kaleshwaram Lift Irrigation Project in Telangana), water is lifted hundreds of metres out of the Godavari River through multi-stage series pumps feeding canal networks across dry districts.
- **Water Transmission Network Optimization:** Water resource engineers use hydraulic models (like EPANET) to simulate pump curves intersecting municipal pipe distribution curves across changing diurnal demand patterns.

<!--beat:recap|Recap-->
## Three things to remember

1. **Operating point rule:** System flow and head are determined by where pump curve intersects pipeline curve: $H_{\text{pump}}(Q) = H_{\text{sys}}(Q)$.
2. **Series vs Parallel rule:** Use series for steep friction/high-lift pipelines; use parallel for flat, low-loss pipelines.
3. **Cavitation safety:** Always maintain $\text{NPSH}_a > \text{NPSH}_r + 0.5$ m to prevent destructive vapor collapse.
