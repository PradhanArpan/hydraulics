## Can a rough concrete wall behave as slick as glass?

Rub your hand on a concrete pavement — it feels rough and abrasive. Yet, when water flows very slowly over that exact same concrete surface, the water experiences friction as if the channel were made of polished glass!

## The Laminar Sublayer & Nikuradse Criteria

Even in fully turbulent flow, turbulent fluctuations must die down to zero at a solid wall because fluid cannot move across a solid surface.

Right at the wall, a microscopically thin zone exists where viscous shear stresses dominate over turbulent Reynolds stresses: the **viscous (laminar) sublayer**.

### The Shear Velocity $u_*$ (m/s)

To measure the intensity of shear stress at the wall, engineers define the friction velocity (or shear velocity):

$$u_* = \sqrt{\frac{\tau_0}{\rho}}$$

*In words: a velocity scale derived purely from the wall shear stress and fluid density.*

### Thickness of the Laminar Sublayer $\delta'$ (m)

Experiments show the viscous sublayer extends up to a non-dimensional distance $y^+ = \frac{y\,u_*}{\nu} \approx 11.6$:

$$\delta' = \frac{11.6\,\nu}{u_*}$$

*In words: the thickness of the calm laminar cushion hugging the wall.*

### Nikuradse's Roughness Classification

Let $k$ be the average height of surface roughness grains (equivalent sand grain roughness):

| Regime | Ratio $\frac{k}{\delta'}$ | Roughness Reynolds $\frac{k\,u_*}{\nu}$ | What happens physically? |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Hydrodynamically Smooth** | $\frac{k}{\delta'} < 0.25$ | $\frac{k\,u_*}{\nu} < 4$ | Grains are completely submerged inside the laminar sublayer. Friction depends on Reynolds number, not roughness $k$. |
| **Transition** | $0.25 \le \frac{k}{\delta'} \le 6.0$ | $4 \le \frac{k\,u_*}{\nu} \le 100$ | Grain tips poke out into the turbulent zone. Both viscosity and roughness $k$ contribute to friction. |
| **Hydrodynamically Rough** | $\frac{k}{\delta'} > 6.0$ | $\frac{k\,u_*}{\nu} > 100$ | Grains break completely through the sublayer, shedding vortex wakes. Friction depends purely on roughness $k$ and is independent of viscosity! |

## Derive shear velocity $u_*$

Click through the steps to see why $\sqrt{\tau_0/\rho}$ has the dimensions of velocity.

```steps
say: Wall shear stress tau_0 has units of force per unit area.
eq: [\tau_0] = \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} = \frac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}/\text{s}^2}{\text{m}^2} = \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^2}
why: By definition, shear stress is shear force divided by contact surface area.
---
say: Fluid density rho has units of mass per unit volume.
eq: [\rho] = \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3}
why: Standard SI units of mass density.
---
say: Divide wall shear stress tau_0 by fluid density rho.
eq: \left[ \frac{\tau_0}{\rho} \right] = \frac{\text{kg}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^2)}{\text{kg}/\text{m}^3} = \frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}
why: Cancelling kilograms leaves metres squared per second squared.
---
say: Take the square root of tau_0 / rho.
eq: \left[ \sqrt{\frac{\tau_0}{\rho}} \right] = \sqrt{\frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}} = \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}
why: Taking the square root directly yields velocity units (m/s). We name this scale shear velocity u_*.
```

## Classifying a concrete flume bed

> [!note] Problem
> Water ($\rho = 1000\ \text{kg}/\text{m}^3$, $\nu = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$) flows through a rectangular concrete canal with average bed roughness grain size $k = 0.50$ mm.
> The measured bed shear stress is $\tau_0 = 4.0\ \text{N}/\text{m}^2$.
> 1. Find the shear velocity $u_*$.
> 2. Calculate the thickness of the laminar sublayer $\delta'$.
> 3. Determine the ratio $k/\delta'$ and classify the bed as hydrodynamically smooth, transitional, or rough.

**Step 1. Shear velocity $u_*$:**
$$u_* = \sqrt{\frac{\tau_0}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{4.0}{1000}} = \sqrt{0.004} = 0.06325\ \text{m}/\text{s} = 6.325\ \text{cm}/\text{s}$$

**Step 2. Laminar sublayer thickness $\delta'$:**
$$\delta' = \frac{11.6\,\nu}{u_*} = \frac{11.6 \times 1.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.06325} = 1.834 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.183\ \text{mm}$$

**Step 3. Check roughness ratio $k/\delta'$:**
$$\frac{k}{\delta'} = \frac{0.50\ \text{mm}}{0.183\ \text{mm}} = 2.73$$

Alternatively, check the roughness Reynolds number:
$$\frac{k\,u_*}{\nu} = \frac{0.0005 \times 0.06325}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 31.6$$

**Step 4. Classification:**
Since $0.25 \le 2.73 \le 6.0$ (and $4 \le 31.6 \le 100$), the boundary lies in the **transitional regime**.

> [!note] What to take away
> The roughness peaks ($0.5$ mm) poke through the thin laminar cushion ($0.18$ mm), but are not yet tall enough to completely dominate the flow. Both viscous drag and form drag act together.

## Civil engineering connections

- **Water Pipe Ageing:** Old cast iron municipal water mains accumulate tuberculation and scale deposits over decades. As roughness $k$ increases, the pipe switches from hydrodynamically smooth to rough, requiring up to 3 times more pumping power!
- **River Bed Armor Layer:** In natural gravel rivers, the laminar sublayer is typically fractions of a millimeter, while river cobbles are 50 to 100 mm. River beds are almost always in the fully rough regime.

<!--beat:recap|Recap-->
## Three things to remember

1. **Viscous sublayer $\delta'$:** The calm laminar layer of thickness $\delta' = \frac{11.6 \nu}{u_*}$ immediately adjacent to the wall.
2. **Shear velocity $u_* = \sqrt{\tau_0/\rho}$:** The velocity scale of wall friction.
3. **Smooth vs Rough:** A boundary is hydrodynamically smooth if roughness grains are buried inside the sublayer ($k < 0.25 \delta'$), and rough if they project completely through ($k > 6 \delta'$).
