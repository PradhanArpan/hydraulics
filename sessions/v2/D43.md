## Designing spillways without pouring concrete

Before 1990, if an engineer wanted to know whether a new dam spillway would suck air, oscillate, or cavitate, they had only one reliable path:
They hired carpenters and masons to build a physical 1:50 scale wooden or acrylic model in a hydraulics laboratory.
Water was pumped through it, and tiny manometers measured water pressures.

Physical models are accurate, but they cost millions of rupees, take months to build, and cannot be resized with a keystroke.
Today, **Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)** lets us build the exact same spillway inside a workstation.

```predict

o: SWE only works for salt water
```

Can a computer accurately capture the violent churning of a hydraulic jump, the curved nappe falling over an ogee weir, or the cavitation pocket under a sluice gate?
Let's see how modern multi-phase free-surface solvers make this possible.

## Free-Surface Hydrodynamics & The VOF Method

When water flows over a spillway into air, we have a two-phase flow problem: **water** below, and **air** above.
The surface separating them is free to move, deform, and break into droplets.

### The Volume of Fluid (VOF) Method
Developed by Hirt and Nichols (1981), the VOF technique tracks the moving free boundary on a fixed Eulerian grid by defining a phase fraction field $\alpha$:
$$\alpha = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{Grid cell is completely filled with water} \\
0 & \text{Grid cell is completely filled with air} \\
0 < \alpha < 1 & \text{Grid cell contains the free water surface interface}
\end{cases}$$

The transport equation for the phase fraction is solved alongside Navier-Stokes momentum:
$$\frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\alpha \mathbf{u}) = 0$$

Effective mixture density and viscosity in interface cells are computed as:
$$\rho_m = \alpha \rho_w + (1 - \alpha) \rho_a, \quad \mu_m = \alpha \mu_w + (1 - \alpha) \mu_a$$

### Ogee Spillway Crest Hydrodynamics
The profile of an ogee crest conforms to the lower nappe of a ventilated sharp-crested weir discharging at design head $H_d$:
- When actual head $H = H_d$: Pressure along the crest is **atmospheric** ($p = 0$).
- When $H < H_d$: Positive gauge pressures push down on the crest ($p > 0$), reducing discharge coefficient.
- When $H > H_d$: Flow pulls away from the crest; negative (sub-atmospheric) gauge pressures develop ($p < 0$).
  If negative pressures drop below water vapor pressure ($p_{\text{vap}} \approx -10\text{ m}$ of water), **cavitation pitting** destroys the concrete surface!

## Energy balance & crest pressure calculation

Follow how Bernoulli's principle reveals sub-atmospheric suction on a spillway crest.

```steps
say: Write total energy head available in the reservoir upstream of the spillway crest.
eq: E = H_{\text{total}} = z_{\text{res}} - z_{\text{crest}}
why: Neglecting approach velocity in a deep reservoir, total head equals water surface elevation above crest.
---
say: Write the Bernoulli energy equation at the spillway crest where elevation z = 0.
eq: E = 0 + y + \frac{p}{\gamma} + \frac{V^2}{2g}
why: Total energy at the crest consists of depth, pressure head, and velocity head.
---
say: Rearrange for the gauge pressure head on the concrete crest surface.
eq: \frac{p}{\gamma} = H_{\text{total}} - y - \frac{V^2}{2g}
why: If velocity head plus water depth exceeds reservoir total head, pressure head must become negative!
---
say: Check for cavitation inception against water vapor pressure.
eq: \frac{p}{\gamma} > -7.5\text{ to } -10\text{ m of water}
why: Vapor pressure of water at ambient temperatures is approximately -101.3 kPa gauge, or -10 m of water head!
```

## Cavitation risk assessment on an ogee spillway

> [!note] Problem
> An ogee spillway has a design head of $H_d = 4.0\text{ m}$.
> During an extreme flood event, the reservoir rises to $H = 5.2\text{ m}$.
> At the spillway crest, flow velocity reaches $V = 9.8\text{ m/s}$.
> 1. Compute the discharge intensity $q$ over the crest ($C = 2.20$ for ogee crest).
> 2. Calculate the gauge pressure head $p/\gamma$ on the concrete crest.
> 3. Check whether the spillway is in danger of cavitation.

**Step 1. Compute discharge intensity $q$:**
$$q = C \, H^{3/2} = 2.20 \times (5.2)^{1.5} = 2.20 \times 11.858 = 26.09\text{ m}^3/\text{s per meter width}$$

**Step 2. Compute depth and pressure at the crest:**
Flow depth at the crest:
$$y = \frac{q}{V} = \frac{26.09}{9.8} = 2.662\text{ m}$$
Velocity head:
$$\frac{V^2}{2g} = \frac{(9.8)^2}{2 \times 9.81} = \frac{96.04}{19.62} = 4.895\text{ m}$$
Apply energy equation from reservoir ($E = 5.20\text{ m}$) to crest:
$$5.20 = 0 + y + \frac{p}{\gamma} + \frac{V^2}{2g} = 2.662 + \frac{p}{\gamma} + 4.895$$
$$\frac{p}{\gamma} = 5.20 - 2.662 - 4.895 = -2.357\text{ m of water}$$

**Step 3. Cavitation safety check:**
$$p = 9.81\text{ kN/m}^3 \times (-2.357\text{ m}) = -23.12\text{ kPa (gauge)}$$
Absolute pressure:
$$p_{\text{abs}} = 101.3 - 23.12 = 78.18\text{ kPa} \gg p_{\text{vap}} \approx 2.34\text{ kPa}$$
$$\therefore \text{Negative gauge pressure } (-2.36\text{ m}) \text{ is safe; well above the } -7.5\text{ m cavitation limit.}$$

## Civil engineering connections

- **Baffle Block Dynamic Drag Forces:** In dam stilling basins, 3D CFD models directly compute the fluctuating dynamic drag and uplift forces on concrete baffle blocks, preventing foundation anchor bolts from shearing during 100-year floods.
- **Aerator Chute Design:** When chute velocities exceed $25\text{ m/s}$, negative pressures cause catastrophic cavitation. Engineers use CFD to design aeration ramps (air slots) that entrain $8-10\%$ air into the boundary layer, completely preventing cavitation damage.

<!--beat:recap|Recap-->
## Three things to remember

1. **Non-hydrostatic physics:** Hydraulic structures require 3D Navier-Stokes or VOF solvers because vertical accelerations cannot be ignored.
2. **The VOF phase fraction:** $\alpha = 1$ (water), $\alpha = 0$ (air), tracking complex splashing and plunging jets.
3. **Cavitation limit:** Crest suction must remain well above $-7.5\text{ to } -10\text{ m}$ of water head to prevent concrete pitting.
