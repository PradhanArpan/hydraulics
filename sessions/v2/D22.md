## Mastering the numerical exam patterns

In engineering exams and field practice, problems on the impact of jets always boil down to three key decisions:
1. Is the target **stationary** or **moving**?
2. Is the surface **flat** or **curved**?
3. Is it a **single plate** or a **continuous series on a wheel**?

```predict

o: Use rho * a * V because it is a wheel (series of vanes) intercepting the entire jet discharge
o: Use zero
a: 2
why: When vanes are mounted on a wheel or turbine runner, successive vanes cut into the jet continuously, capturing 100% of the jet's mass flow rate: m_dot = rho * a * V.
```

## The 5-step problem solving roadmap

Before plugging numbers into a calculator, follow this standardized five-step template:

```
Step 1: Compute Jet Area & Flow Rate
        a = (pi/4) * d^2,   Q = a * V,   m_dot = rho * Q

Step 2: Determine Relative Velocity (if moving)
        V_r1 = V_1 - u   (for tangential entry)

Step 3: Resolve Velocity Components (Inlet & Outlet)
        Find V_w1, V_w2, V_f1, V_f2 from velocity triangles

Step 4: Compute Driving Force & Power
        F_x = m_dot * (V_w1 +- V_w2)
        P = F_x * u

Step 5: Check Efficiency & Limits
        eta = P / (0.5 * m_dot * V_1^2) <= 100%
```

## Decision tree for jet impact problems

Click through the decision path to classify any problem correctly.

```steps
say: Step 1: Check plate motion. Does the problem state the plate is stationary or moving?
eq: u = 0 \quad (\text{Stationary}) \quad \text{vs} \quad u > 0 \quad (\text{Moving})
why: If stationary, work done is identically zero. If moving, power = F * u.
---
say: Step 2: If moving, check whether it is a single plate or a series of vanes on a wheel.
eq: \dot{m}_{\text{single}} = \rho\,a\,(V - u) \quad \text{vs} \quad \dot{m}_{\text{wheel}} = \rho\,a\,V
why: A single plate runs away from the jet; a wheel continuously intercepts the entire nozzle discharge.
---
say: Step 3: Check plate geometry. Is it flat or curved?
eq: \text{Flat: } \Delta V_x = (V - u) \quad \text{vs} \quad \text{Curved: } \Delta V_x = V_{w1} \pm V_{w2}
why: Curved vanes redirect relative velocity backward, dramatically boosting momentum transfer.
---
say: Step 4: Check for surface friction. Does the problem state smooth or a friction factor k?
eq: V_{r2} = V_{r1} \quad (\text{Smooth, } k=1.0) \quad \text{vs} \quad V_{r2} = k\,V_{r1} \quad (\text{Friction})
why: Friction reduces relative exit speed along the blade by factor k.
```

## Clinic Problem: Symmetrical curved vane on a rotating wheel

> [!note] Problem
> A jet of water $75$ mm in diameter having a velocity of $20$ m/s strikes a series of symmetrical curved vanes mounted on a wheel. The vanes are moving at $10$ m/s in the same direction as the jet.
> The jet enters tangentially without shock. The water leaves the vanes at an angle of $20^\circ$ to the direction of motion. Assuming smooth vanes ($k = 1.0$), calculate:
> 1. Vane inlet angle $\theta$
> 2. Force exerted by the jet in the direction of motion
> 3. Power developed by the wheel
> 4. Hydraulic efficiency of the wheel

**Step 1. Jet area and mass flow rate:**
$$a = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.075)^2 = 4.4179 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$$
Since it is a series of vanes on a wheel:
$$\dot{m} = \rho\,a\,V_1 = 1000 \times 4.4179 \times 10^{-3} \times 20 = 88.36\ \text{kg}/\text{s}$$

**Step 2. Inlet velocity triangle:**
The jet enters tangentially ($\alpha_1 = 0^\circ$):
- Inlet whirl velocity: $V_{w1} = V_1 = 20\ \text{m}/\text{s}$
- Inlet relative velocity: $V_{r1} = V_1 - u = 20 - 10 = 10\ \text{m}/\text{s}$
- Inlet vane angle: $\theta = 0^\circ$ (tangential entry)

**Step 3. Outlet velocity triangle:**
Since the vane is smooth, $V_{r2} = V_{r1} = 10$ m/s.
The outlet vane angle is $\beta_2 = 20^\circ$:
$$V_{r2x} = V_{r2} \cos(20^\circ) = 10 \times 0.9397 = 9.397\ \text{m}/\text{s} \quad (\text{backward relative to blade})$$
Outlet whirl velocity in direction of blade motion:
$$V_{w2} = u - V_{r2x} = 10 - 9.397 = +0.603\ \text{m}/\text{s} \quad (\text{forward direction})$$

Change in whirl velocity:
$$\Delta V_w = V_{w1} - V_{w2} = 20 - 0.603 = 19.397\ \text{m}/\text{s}$$
*(Alternatively, in standard notation where $V_{w2}$ is written as $V_{r2}\cos\beta - u$, $\Delta V_w = V_{w1} + V_{w2}' = 10 + 9.397 = 19.397$ m/s)*

**Step 4. Force and Power:**
$$F_x = \dot{m} \cdot \Delta V_w = 88.36 \times 19.397 = 1713.9\ \text{N} = 1.714\ \text{kN}$$
$$P = F_x \cdot u = 1713.9 \times 10 = 17{,}139\ \text{W} = 17.14\ \text{kW}$$

**Step 5. Hydraulic efficiency:**
$$P_{\text{jet}} = \frac{1}{2}\dot{m}\,V_1^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 88.36 \times (20)^2 = 17{,}672\ \text{W} = 17.67\ \text{kW}$$
$$\eta_h = \frac{17.14}{17.67} = 0.970 = 97.0\%$$

> [!note] What to take away
> Because $u = 10$ m/s is exactly $V_1/2$, and the deflection angle is nearly $180^\circ$ ($160^\circ$), almost all kinetic energy of the jet is transferred to the wheel ($97\%$ efficiency)!

```quiz
q: In a question where a jet strikes a single moving plate, which term is squared in the force formula?
o: V^2
o: u^2
o: (V - u)^2
a: 3
why: For a single moving plate, m_dot = rho * a * (V - u) and delta V = (V - u), giving F = rho * a * (V - u)^2.
---
q: If the relative velocity drops from 20 m/s at inlet to 16 m/s at outlet, what is the vane friction factor k?
o: k = 1.25
o: k = 0.80
o: k = 0.50
a: 2
why: k = V_r2 / V_r1 = 16 / 20 = 0.80.
```

## Three things to remember

1. **Wheel intercepts full discharge:** Use $\dot{m} = \rho a V$ for wheels, but $\dot{m} = \rho a (V - u)$ for single plates.
2. **Optimal speed ratio:** Curved vanes on a wheel achieve over $90\%$ efficiency when blade speed $u \approx 0.45$ to $0.48 V_1$.
3. **Five-step method:** Always calculate area $\to$ relative velocities $\to$ whirl components $\to$ power $\to$ efficiency.
