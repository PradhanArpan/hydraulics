## The rhythmic heartbeat of a piston

Walk past a vintage rural tube-well or a high-pressure concrete grouting rig. You hear a steady, rhythmic mechanical thumping: *chug-clack, chug-clack*.

A piston moves back and forth inside a cylinder. Unlike a continuous centrifugal pump, this machine squirts water in pulsating bursts. Why are positive displacement pumps still essential when centrifugal pumps exist?

## Reciprocating Pump Mechanics

A reciprocating pump is a **positive displacement machine**:
- A rotating crank of radius $r$ drives a connecting rod of length $L$, moving a piston back and forth with stroke $S = 2 r$.
- Piston displacement from inner dead center (IDC):
  $$x = r(1 - \cos\theta)$$
- Instantaneous piston velocity (assuming simple harmonic motion):
  $$v = \frac{dx}{dt} = \omega\,r \sin\theta$$

### 1. Theoretical Discharge $Q_{\text{th}}$
- **Single-Acting Pump (delivers once per crank revolution):**
  $$Q_{\text{th}} = \frac{A \cdot L \cdot N}{60} \quad (\text{where } L = 2r \text{ is stroke length, } A = \frac{\pi}{4}D^2)$$
- **Double-Acting Pump (delivers on both forward and return strokes):**
  $$Q_{\text{th}} = \frac{(2 A - a_r) \cdot L \cdot N}{60} \approx \frac{2\,A \cdot L \cdot N}{60}$$

### 2. Slip and Negative Slip
- **Slip ($S$):** The difference between theoretical displacement and actual delivered volume:
  $$\text{Slip} = Q_{\text{th}} - Q_{\text{act}}$$
  $$\% \text{ Slip} = \frac{Q_{\text{th}} - Q_{\text{act}}}{Q_{\text{th}}} \times 100\%$$
- **Coefficient of Discharge $C_d$:**
  $$C_d = \frac{Q_{\text{act}}}{Q_{\text{th}}} = 1 - \frac{\text{Slip}}{100}$$
- **Negative Slip:** Occurs when actual discharge exceeds theoretical discharge ($Q_{\text{act}} > Q_{\text{th}}$, so $\text{Slip} < 0$ and $C_d > 1$). This happens in pumps with **long suction pipes and low delivery heads running at high speed**, where the inertia of moving suction water forces both valves open simultaneously!

### 3. Acceleration Head & Air Vessels
Because the piston accelerates and decelerates cyclically ($\sin\theta$), water inside the pipes must accelerate, creating an inertial acceleration head:

$$h_a = \frac{l}{g} \frac{A}{a} \omega^2\,r \cos\theta$$

To eliminate acceleration head and pulsating friction losses:
- An **Air Vessel** is a closed cast-iron dome fitted near the cylinder on the suction and delivery pipes.
- The compressed air pocket acts like a hydraulic shock absorber: it absorbs water during peak piston velocity ($v > v_{\text{mean}}$) and releases water back during dead centers ($v < v_{\text{mean}}$).
- **Work Saved Against Pipe Friction:**
  - **Single-acting pump:** Fitting an air vessel saves **$84.8\%$** of friction work!
  - **Double-acting pump:** Saves **$39.2\%$** of friction work!

## Derive the 84.8% work saved by an air vessel

Click through the integration of friction power.

```steps
say: In a single-acting pump without air vessel, water velocity in the delivery pipe varies as a sine wave during half the cycle.
eq: v = \frac{A}{a}\omega\,r \sin\theta \quad \text{for } 0 \le \theta \le \pi, \quad v = 0 \quad \text{for } \pi \le \theta \le 2\pi
why: Simple harmonic piston motion reflected into pipe area a.
---
say: Pipe friction loss is proportional to velocity squared (Darcy-Weisbach).
eq: h_f = \frac{4 f l v^2}{2 g d} = \frac{4 f l}{2 g d}\left(\frac{A}{a}\omega\,r\right)^2 \sin^2\theta
why: Classical pipe friction equation with pulsating velocity v.
---
say: Average work done against friction per cycle without air vessel:
eq: W_{\text{without}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi \left[\frac{4 f l}{2 g d}\left(\frac{A}{a}\omega\,r\right)^2 \sin^2\theta\right] d\theta = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot C = \frac{1}{4} C
why: Integrating sin^2(theta) from 0 to pi gives pi/2.
---
say: With an air vessel, discharge in the pipe beyond the vessel becomes completely steady at mean velocity.
eq: v_{\text{mean}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi \left(\frac{A}{a}\omega\,r\sin\theta\right) d\theta = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{A}{a}\omega\,r\right)
why: The air vessel evens out flow over the entire 360 degree revolution.
---
say: Work done with steady mean flow over the whole cycle:
eq: W_{\text{with}} = \frac{4 f l}{2 g d} v_{\text{mean}}^2 = \left(\frac{1}{\pi}\right)^2 C = \frac{1}{\pi^2} C
why: Steady velocity v_mean produces constant friction head.
---
say: Percentage work saved:
eq: \% \text{ Saved} = \frac{W_{\text{without}} - W_{\text{with}}}{W_{\text{without}}} = \frac{\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{\pi^2}}{\frac{1}{4}} = 1 - \frac{4}{\pi^2} = 1 - 0.4053 = 84.8\%!
why: Fitting an air vessel saves exactly 84.8% of pipe friction power!
```

## Sizing a double-acting reciprocating pump

> [!note] Problem
> A double-acting reciprocating pump has a cylinder diameter $D = 200$ mm and stroke length $L = 300$ mm.
> The pump runs at $N = 45$ rpm and discharges water against a total head of $40$ m ($h_s = 4.0$ m, $h_d = 36.0$ m).
> The actual measured discharge is $18.0$ litres per second.
> 1. Calculate the theoretical discharge $Q_{\text{th}}$ in L/s
> 2. Determine the percentage slip and coefficient of discharge $C_d$
> 3. Calculate the power required to drive the pump assuming an overall mechanical efficiency $\eta_o = 80\%$

**Step 1. Cross-sectional area of cylinder:**
$$A = \frac{\pi}{4} D^2 = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.20)^2 = 0.031416\ \text{m}^2$$

**Step 2. Theoretical discharge $Q_{\text{th}}$ (double-acting):**
$$Q_{\text{th}} = \frac{2\,A\,L\,N}{60} = \frac{2 \times 0.031416 \times 0.30 \times 45}{60} = \frac{0.84823}{60} = 0.014137\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} = 14.14\ \text{L}/\text{s}$$

*(Wait, notice that actual discharge $Q_{\text{act}} = 18.0$ L/s is greater than $Q_{\text{th}} = 14.14$ L/s! This indicates negative slip!)*

**Step 3. Slip and Coefficient of Discharge:**
$$\text{Slip} = Q_{\text{th}} - Q_{\text{act}} = 14.14 - 18.0 = -3.86\ \text{L}/\text{s}$$
$$\% \text{ Slip} = \frac{14.14 - 18.0}{14.14} \times 100\% = \frac{-3.86}{14.14} \times 100\% = -27.30\%\ \text{(Negative Slip!)}$$
$$C_d = \frac{Q_{\text{act}}}{Q_{\text{th}}} = \frac{18.0}{14.14} = 1.273$$

**Step 4. Power required to drive pump:**
$$\text{Water power } P_w = \rho\,g\,Q_{\text{act}}\,H = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.018 \times 40 = 7063.2\ \text{W} = 7.06\ \text{kW}$$
$$\text{Motor shaft power } P = \frac{P_w}{\eta_o} = \frac{7.06}{0.80} = 8.83\ \text{kW}$$

> [!note] What to take away
> Negative slip occurs when high fluid momentum in the suction pipe rams past the open suction valve during the delivery stroke, filling the cylinder with more than $100\%$ capacity!

## Civil engineering connections

- **Grout Injection Pumps in Dam Foundations:** In dam construction, cement-bentonite grout must be injected under pressures exceeding $50$ atmospheres into micro-fissures in rock foundations. Positive displacement reciprocating plunger pumps provide this immense high-pressure thrust where centrifugal pumps fail.
- **Dosing Pumps in Water Treatment:** Chemical treatment plants require exact micro-dosing of alum and chlorine (e.g. precisely $12.5$ mL/min). Metering reciprocating diaphragm pumps deliver exact calibrated volumetric doses independent of pipeline pressure.

## Three things to remember

1. **Positive displacement rule:** Trapped volume $Q_{\text{th}} = \frac{A L N}{60}$ (single) or $\frac{2 A L N}{60}$ (double) is forced forward mechanically.
2. **Slip meaning:** Positive slip indicates valve leakage; negative slip occurs when suction inertia overfills the cylinder.
3. **Air vessel advantage:** Eliminates acceleration head shock and saves up to $84.8\%$ of pipe friction work.
