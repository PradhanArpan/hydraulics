## Backward, radial, or forward?

If you were asked to design an impeller blade to fling water out with the highest possible speed, you might be tempted to curve the blade **forward** like a scoop.

Yet, if you cut open any commercial water pump made anywhere in the world, the blades curve **backward** away from the direction of rotation! Why do hydraulic engineers deliberately point the blade tips backward?

```predict
q: Why do commercial centrifugal pumps use backward-curved impeller vanes (beta_2 < 90 degrees) instead of forward-curved vanes?
o: Backward blades are easier to paint
o: Backward-curved vanes produce a stable drooping head-flow curve with self-limiting motor power, preventing motor burnout when discharge surges!
o: Forward blades rotate backward
a: 2
why: Forward-curved vanes cause power demand to skyrocket uncontrollably as flow increases, overloading and burning out the electric motor. Backward vanes produce a stable, self-limiting power curve that protects the motor!
```

Today you will see the velocity triangles of a centrifugal pump impeller and how to calculate its **manometric head and work done**.

## Pump Heads, Work Done & Efficiencies

### 1. The Three Heads of a Pump
- **Static Head $H_{\text{stat}}$ (m):** The vertical lift from sump level to delivery tank level:
  $$H_{\text{stat}} = h_s + h_d$$
- **Manometric Head $H_m$ (m):** Total head against which the pump actually works (static lift plus all pipe friction and exit velocity head):

$$H_m = h_s + h_d + h_{fs} + h_{fd} + \frac{V_d^2}{2g}$$

*In words: static suction and delivery lift plus pipe friction losses plus kinetic energy discharging into the tank.*

- **Euler Head (Theoretical Head) $H_e$ (m):** Head imparted to water by the rotating impeller vanes:

$$H_e = \frac{V_{w2}\,u_2}{g} \quad (\text{assuming radial entry at inlet: } V_{w1} = 0)$$

### 2. Impeller Outlet Velocity Triangle
At the impeller outer exit (diameter $D_2$, width $b_2$):
- Peripheral speed: $u_2 = \frac{\pi\,D_2\,N}{60}$
- Flow velocity (radial throughput): $V_{f2} = \frac{Q}{\pi\,D_2\,b_2}$
- Whirl velocity:
  $$V_{w2} = u_2 - \frac{V_{f2}}{\tan\beta_2}$$

*Notice that as flow $Q$ (and thus $V_{f2}$) increases:*
- For **backward vanes ($\beta_2 < 90^\circ$, $\tan\beta_2 > 0$):** $V_{w2}$ decreases, causing head to droop stably!
- For **forward vanes ($\beta_2 > 90^\circ$, $\tan\beta_2 < 0$):** $V_{w2}$ increases, causing head to climb unstably!

### 3. Pump Efficiencies
1. **Manometric Efficiency $\eta_{\text{mano}}$:**
   $$\eta_{\text{mano}} = \frac{g\,H_m}{V_{w2}\,u_2}$$
2. **Mechanical Efficiency $\eta_m$:**
   $$\eta_m = \frac{\text{Impeller Power}}{\text{Motor Shaft Power}} = \frac{\rho\,Q\,(V_{w2}\,u_2)}{P_{\text{shaft}}}$$
3. **Overall Efficiency $\eta_o$:**
   $$\eta_o = \eta_{\text{mano}} \cdot \eta_m = \frac{\rho\,g\,Q\,H_m}{P_{\text{shaft}}}$$

$$\text{Motor Shaft Power Required } P = \frac{\rho\,g\,Q\,H_m}{\eta_o}$$

## Derive why backward vanes ensure stable pumping

Click through the mathematical proof of the drooping $H-Q$ curve.

```steps
say: From the Euler head equation with radial entry at the impeller eye (Vw1 = 0).
eq: H_e = \frac{V_{w2}\,u_2}{g}
why: Classical momentum balance for centrifugal turbomachines.
---
say: From the exit velocity triangle, resolve whirl velocity Vw2 in terms of blade angle beta2.
eq: V_{w2} = u_2 - V_{f2}\cot\beta_2
why: Adjacent side of relative velocity right triangle.
---
say: Substitute flow velocity Vf2 = Q / (pi * D2 * b2).
eq: V_{w2} = u_2 - \left(\frac{Q}{\pi\,D_2\,b_2}\right)\cot\beta_2
why: Flow velocity is volumetric discharge Q divided by peripheral cylindrical area.
---
say: Substitute Vw2 into the Euler head equation.
eq: H_e = \frac{u_2^2}{g} - \left(\frac{u_2\cot\beta_2}{g\,\pi\,D_2\,b_2}\right) Q = C_1 - C_2\,Q
why: For backward vanes (beta2 < 90 deg), cot(beta2) is strictly POSITIVE, so head decreases linearly as discharge Q rises.
---
say: This negative slope (dH/dQ < 0) provides automatic self-regulation.
eq: \frac{dH}{dQ} < 0 \implies \text{Stable operating point with system curve!}
why: If a momentary surge in flow occurs, pump head drops, naturally throttling the system back to equilibrium!
```

## Impeller design & power calculation

> [!note] Problem
> A centrifugal pump impeller has an external diameter $D_2 = 350$ mm and width at exit $b_2 = 30$ mm.
> The pump runs at $N = 1450$ rpm and delivers $Q = 0.050\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$ ($50$ L/s) against a manometric head $H_m = 28$ m.
> The impeller vanes are curved backward at an angle $\beta_2 = 30^\circ$. Manometric efficiency is $\eta_{\text{mano}} = 80\%$ and mechanical efficiency is $\eta_m = 90\%$. Calculate:
> 1. Peripheral velocity $u_2$ and flow velocity $V_{f2}$ at exit
> 2. Whirl velocity at outlet $V_{w2}$
> 3. Shaft power required to drive the pump in kW

**Step 1. Peripheral velocity $u_2$:**
$$u_2 = \frac{\pi\,D_2\,N}{60} = \frac{\pi \times 0.350 \times 1450}{60} = \frac{1594.36}{60} = 26.57\ \text{m}/\text{s}$$

**Step 2. Flow velocity $V_{f2}$ at exit:**
$$A_2 = \pi\,D_2\,b_2 = \pi \times 0.350 \times 0.030 = 0.03299\ \text{m}^2$$
$$V_{f2} = \frac{Q}{A_2} = \frac{0.050}{0.03299} = 1.516\ \text{m}/\text{s}$$

**Step 3. Outlet whirl velocity $V_{w2}$:**
$$\beta_2 = 30^\circ \implies \tan(30^\circ) = 0.5774$$
$$V_{w2} = u_2 - \frac{V_{f2}}{\tan\beta_2} = 26.57 - \frac{1.516}{0.5774} = 26.57 - 2.625 = 23.945\ \text{m}/\text{s} \approx 23.95\ \text{m}/\text{s}$$

**Step 4. Verification with manometric efficiency:**
$$\eta_{\text{mano}} = \frac{g\,H_m}{V_{w2}\,u_2} \implies H_m = \frac{\eta_{\text{mano}}\,V_{w2}\,u_2}{g} = \frac{0.80 \times 23.945 \times 26.57}{9.81} = \frac{508.97}{9.81} = 51.88\ \text{m}$$
*(Note: At this speed and blade angle, the impeller can generate up to $51.9$ m of manometric head).*

**Step 5. Shaft power required:**
$$\text{Water power } P_w = \rho\,g\,Q\,H_m = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.050 \times 28 = 13{,}734\ \text{W} = 13.73\ \text{kW}$$
$$\text{Overall efficiency } \eta_o = \eta_{\text{mano}} \times \eta_m = 0.80 \times 0.90 = 0.72 = 72\%$$
$$\text{Shaft power } P_{\text{shaft}} = \frac{P_w}{\eta_o} = \frac{13.73}{0.72} = 19.07\ \text{kW}$$

> [!note] What to take away
> Sizing the electric motor requires $19.1$ kW (a standard $22$ kW commercial motor would be selected), providing safe continuous pumping without thermal overload!

## Civil engineering connections

- **Municipal Booster Pumping Stations:** Water boards operate large centrifugal pumping mains supplying clean water to overhead balancing reservoirs. Selecting backward-curved impellers ensures that when demand drops at night, the motor power automatically drops, saving millions in municipal electricity bills!
- **Fire Fighting Pumps:** Fire hydrant pumps must operate under NFPA standards requiring a smooth, continuously drooping $H-Q$ curve to prevent pressure surges that could burst fire hoses when hydrants are opened or closed.

<!--beat:recap|Recap-->
## Three things to remember

1. **Manometric head formula:** $H_m = h_s + h_d + h_{fs} + h_{fd} + \frac{V_d^2}{2g}$ is the true total head generated by the pump.
2. **Backward vane advantage:** $\beta_2 < 90^\circ$ produces stable drooping $H-Q$ curves and self-limiting power curves, preventing motor burnout.
3. **Shaft power formula:** $P_{\text{shaft}} = \frac{\rho g Q H_m}{\eta_o}$ determines motor sizing.
