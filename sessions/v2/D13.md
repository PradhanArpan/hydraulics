## A bump on the bed: does the water surface go up?

A calm, deep stream flows along a channel. Someone raises the bed with a low concrete hump. Nothing is added to the stream and nothing is taken away.

```predict
q: Calm (subcritical) water meets a low hump on the channel bed. What does the water surface do as it passes over the hump?
o: It rises, because the bed rose
o: It dips slightly
o: It stays exactly level
a: 2
why: The hump "costs" energy. The stream has no extra energy to pay with, so the water gets shallower and faster, and the surface sinks. It feels backwards, and it is true only for calm flow. For fast flow it is the other way round.
```

Today's idea is one rule: **no energy is lost, so any energy spent on a bump or a narrowing must come out of the water's own depth and speed.**

## The energy budget

Without friction, the total energy line stays level. Over a hump of height $\Delta z$ the water has less energy left for depth and speed:

$$E_2 = E_1 - \Delta z$$

*In words: whatever the hump takes, the water gives up.*

On the $E$–$y$ diagram from Day 12, the hump slides the water's state to the left by $\Delta z$. A narrowing does not change $E$, but the same flow now squeezes through a smaller width, so each metre of width carries more flow ($q$ goes up). That moves the water onto a different curve.

| | Calm flow ($Fr < 1$) | Fast flow ($Fr > 1$) |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Hump** | Depth falls, speed rises. The surface **dips**. | Depth rises, speed falls. The surface **rises**. |
| **Narrowing** | Depth falls, speed rises. | Depth rises, speed falls. |

**Choking** happens when the transition asks for more than the water has. The water cannot go below the critical state. Calm flow then **piles up upstream** (the rise is called *afflux*) and passes the transition at critical depth.

> [!note] A word about specific force
> When a jump is involved, energy is lost, so we cannot use $E$ across it. Instead we use the **specific force**
> $$M = \frac{Q^2}{gA} + A\bar{y} \qquad\left(\text{per metre width: } \frac{q^2}{g\,y} + \frac{y^2}{2}\right)$$
> *In words: the push of the flow, made of its momentum plus the water pressure.* It is the same before and after a jump. You will use it tomorrow.

## How high can the hump be?

```steps
say: Before the hump the specific energy is $E_1$. Over the hump, with no losses, it drops by the height of the hump.
eq: E_2 = E_1 - \Delta z
why: The bed rose by $\Delta z$, so that much energy is now "spent" on climbing.
---
say: The least energy the flow can have is the critical energy $E_c$ (Day 12).
eq: E_c = 1.5\,y_c = 1.5\left(\frac{q^2}{g}\right)^{1/3}
why: Below this, no depth can carry the flow.
---
say: So over the hump we need $E_2 \ge E_c$. Put in $E_2 = E_1 - \Delta z$ and solve for $\Delta z$.
eq: \Delta z_{max} = E_1 - E_c
why: At exactly this height the flow goes over the top at critical depth. Any higher and it chokes.
---
say: For a narrowing, find the largest flow per metre width that energy $E$ can carry. At critical flow $y_c = 2E/3$ and $q = \sqrt{g\,y_c^3}$.
eq: q_{max} = \sqrt{g}\,\left(\frac{2E}{3}\right)^{3/2}
why: This uses the same critical-flow facts as the hump.
---
say: The total flow is $Q$, so the narrowest width that still works is $Q$ divided by that largest $q$.
eq: b_{min} = \frac{Q}{q_{max}}
why: Narrower than this, the opening cannot pass the flow and the stream chokes.
```

## A hump, then a narrowing

> [!note] Problem
> A rectangular channel 3 m wide carries $Q = 6\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$ at a calm depth of $y_1 = 1.5$ m. (a) A 0.20 m hump is built on the bed. Find the depth over it and the largest hump the channel could take. (b) Instead, the channel narrows to 2.0 m wide. Find the depth in the narrow part and the narrowest width allowed.

**Flow per metre width.** $q = 6/3 = 2\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$. Critical depth $y_c = (4/9.81)^{1/3} = 0.742$ m and $E_c = 1.5 \times 0.742 = 1.112$ m.

**Before.** $V_1 = 2/1.5 = 1.33$ m/s, $Fr_1 = 0.35$ (calm). $E_1 = 1.5 + 1.33^2/19.62 = 1.591$ m.

**(a) Hump.**
1. Biggest hump: $\Delta z_{max} = E_1 - E_c = 1.591 - 1.112 = 0.478$ m. The 0.20 m hump is lower, so no choking.
2. Energy over the hump: $E_2 = 1.591 - 0.20 = 1.391$ m.
3. Solve $y_2 + 0.2039/y_2^2 = 1.391$ on the calm side (above $y_c$): $y_2 = 1.263$ m, $V_2 = 1.58$ m/s.
4. Water-surface level over the hump: $0.20 + 1.263 = 1.463$ m. That is **0.04 m below** the upstream level of 1.5 m. The surface dips, as the widget showed.

**(b) Narrowing to 2.0 m.**
1. New flow per metre: $q_2 = 6/2.0 = 3\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$. The energy stays $E_1 = 1.591$ m.
2. Solve $y_2 + 0.4587/y_2^2 = 1.591$ on the calm side: $y_2 = 1.332$ m, $V_2 = 2.25$ m/s, $Fr_2 = 0.62$. Shallower and faster, still calm.
3. Narrowest allowed: $q_{max} = \sqrt{9.81}\,(2 \times 1.591/3)^{3/2} = 3.42\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$, so $b_{min} = 6/3.42 = 1.75$ m. Narrower than 1.75 m and the stream chokes.

> [!note] What to take away
> Both checks are the same idea: compare what the transition asks for with the energy the stream has to spare. The widget uses these same numbers. Set it to calm flow, hump 0.20 m, and compare with step (a).
