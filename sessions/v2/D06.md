## Why do golf balls have dimples, and bridge piers shed violent whirlpools?

If you drop a smooth steel ball into water, it leaves a huge, turbulent wake behind it that drags it back forcefully. But if you dimple the surface like a golf ball, it flies nearly twice as far! Why does a rougher ball experience less overall drag?

```predict
q: When water flows past a widening channel or past the curved back of a circular bridge pier, what causes the water to peel away from the solid surface?
o: The water gets pushed back by rising downstream pressure (adverse pressure gradient) until near-wall fluid comes to a dead stop and reverses
o: Gravity pulls the water away
o: The wall repels the water magnetically
a: 1
why: When flow decelerates, static pressure increases downstream (dp/dx > 0). Slow-moving fluid near the wall runs out of kinetic energy and reverses direction, peeling the boundary layer away from the wall.
```

## The Mechanics of Flow Separation

When a fluid passes around a curved body (like a bridge pier or spillway crest):

1. **Favourable pressure gradient ($dp/dx < 0$):**
   - Pressure drops in the flow direction.
   - Fluid accelerates; the boundary layer stays thin and firmly attached.

2. **Adverse pressure gradient ($dp/dx > 0$):**
   - Pressure rises in the flow direction (e.g. on the expanding rear of a cylinder or in a diverging diffuser).
   - High pressure pushes backwards against the slow-moving fluid inside the boundary layer.

### The Point of Separation

Since fluid right next to the wall has very little kinetic energy (due to no-slip friction), it quickly comes to a complete standstill:

$$\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)_{y=0} = 0 \iff \tau_0 = 0$$

*In words: the exact location where wall shear stress vanishes and flow is on the verge of reversing.*

Past the separation point:
- Fluid flows backwards: $\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)_{y=0} < 0$.
- A large low-pressure wake of swirling vortices forms behind the body.

### Two Components of Total Drag

$$F_{\text{total}} = F_{\text{skin friction}} + F_{\text{form (pressure)}}$$

- **Skin friction drag:** Caused by viscous rubbing along wetted surface area. Dominates on slender, streamlined bodies (airfoils, ship hulls).
- **Form (pressure) drag:** Caused by high pressure in front and low separated wake pressure behind. Dominates on blunt, bluff bodies (bridge piers, circular pipes).

| Body Type | Skin Friction Drag | Form (Pressure) Drag | Drag Coefficient $C_D$ |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Circular Cylinder (Laminar)** | $\approx 2\%$ | $\approx 98\%$ | $\approx 1.20$ |
| **Circular Cylinder (Turbulent / Dimpled)** | $\approx 5\%$ | $\approx 95\%$ | $\approx 0.35$ |
| **Streamlined Airfoil** | $\approx 90\%$ | $\approx 10\%$ | $\approx 0.04$ |

## Why an inflection point must occur before separation

Click through the steps to see the mathematical condition for flow separation.

```steps
say: Write the boundary layer momentum equation right at the solid surface y = 0.
eq: u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dx} + \nu\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}
why: Prandtl's horizontal momentum equation for 2D boundary layer flow.
---
say: At the solid wall, both velocity components are zero due to the no-slip condition: u = 0 and v = 0.
eq: 0 = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dx} + \nu\left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}\right)_{y=0} \implies \mu\left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}\right)_{y=0} = \frac{dp}{dx}
why: Convective acceleration terms vanish completely at the stationary wall.
---
say: In an adverse pressure gradient, dp/dx > 0, so the wall curvature must be positive.
eq: \left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}\right)_{y=0} > 0
why: The second derivative of velocity at the wall has the exact same sign as the pressure gradient.
---
say: Near the outer edge of the boundary layer (y -> delta), the velocity curves back to reach the free-stream value U.
eq: \left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}\right)_{y \to \delta} < 0
why: The velocity profile must be concave downward to merge smoothly with the undisturbed outer flow.
---
say: Since the second derivative is positive at the wall and negative at the outer edge, it must cross zero in between!
eq: \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = 0 \quad \text{at some point } y_{\text{inflection}}
why: By the intermediate value theorem, an inflection point MUST exist in the velocity profile before flow can separate.
```

## Drag on a circular bridge pier vs streamlined pier

> [!note] Problem
> A bridge pier of diameter $D = 1.2$ m and submerged height $H = 4.0$ m stands in a river flowing at $U_\infty = 2.0$ m/s ($\rho = 1000\ \text{kg}/\text{m}^3$).
> 1. Calculate the total drag force $F_D$ on a circular cylindrical pier with drag coefficient $C_D = 1.20$.
> 2. Calculate the drag force if the pier is fitted with a streamlined cutwater casing with $C_D = 0.15$.
> 3. How much drag force is saved by streamlining?

**Step 1. Projected frontal area of the pier:**
$$A_{\text{front}} = D \times H = 1.2 \times 4.0 = 4.80\ \text{m}^2$$

**Step 2. Dynamic pressure of the river flow:**
$$q = \frac{1}{2}\rho\,U_\infty^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 1000 \times (2.0)^2 = 2000\ \text{N}/\text{m}^2 = 2000\ \text{Pa}$$

**Step 3. Drag force on circular pier ($C_D = 1.20$):**
$$F_{\text{circular}} = C_D \cdot A_{\text{front}} \cdot q = 1.20 \times 4.80 \times 2000 = 11{,}520\ \text{N} = 11.52\ \text{kN}$$

**Step 4. Drag force on streamlined pier ($C_D = 0.15$):**
$$F_{\text{streamlined}} = C_D \cdot A_{\text{front}} \cdot q = 0.15 \times 4.80 \times 2000 = 1{,}440\ \text{N} = 1.44\ \text{kN}$$

**Step 5. Reduction in drag force:**
$$\Delta F = 11{,}520 - 1{,}440 = 10{,}080\ \text{N} = 10.08\ \text{kN} \quad (87.5\%\ \text{reduction!})$$

> [!note] What to take away
> Preventing early boundary layer separation drops the hydraulic drag force on the bridge pier by almost $90\%$, drastically reducing scouring pressure on the foundation bed!

## Civil engineering connections

- **Spillway Creep & Separation:** If an ogee spillway crest is built steeper than the natural nappe shape, the water separates from the concrete, forming a dangerous sub-atmospheric vacuum pocket that causes severe cavitation damage and vibration.
- **Bridge Pier Cutwaters:** Adding pointed triangular or rounded cutwaters to upstream and downstream bridge piers prevents massive vortex shedding wakes, suppressing foundation scour.
- **Diffusers & Expansions:** In aqueducts and pump discharge pipes, gradual expansion angles ($< 7^\circ$) prevent diffuser stall and huge head losses.

<!--beat:recap|Recap-->
## Three things to remember

1. **Adverse pressure gradient causes separation:** When $dp/dx > 0$, rising pressure halts slow fluid near the wall.
2. **Separation threshold:** $\tau_0 = \mu \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)_{y=0} = 0$. Past this point, reverse flow sets in.
3. **Form drag vs Friction drag:** Bluff bodies suffer massive form drag due to separated wakes. Streamlining delays separation, reducing total drag by up to $90\%$.
