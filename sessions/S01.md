> [!note] Core Concept
> **Ludwig Prandtl (1904)** demonstrated that for fluids of small viscosity (water and air), viscous frictional resistance is confined entirely to a microscopically thin layer adjacent to solid boundaries—termed the **boundary layer**. Outside this layer, fluid behaves as an ideal, inviscid fluid governed by potential flow theory and Bernoulli's theorem.

## 1. Physical Origin & No-Slip Condition
When a fluid flows over a solid stationary boundary, intermolecular adhesive forces compel the fluid molecules in direct contact with the wall to adhere to it:
$$u(x, y=0) = 0$$
At a finite distance normal to the wall ($y = \delta$), velocity merges with the undisturbed potential flow velocity $U_\infty$. This steep velocity gradient across $\delta(x)$ produces significant shear stresses:
$$\tau = \mu \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$$

## 2. Growth and Regimes along a Flat Plate
As fluid proceeds downstream along a flat plate:
1. **Laminar Zone ($Re_x < 5 \times 10^5$):** Flow is smooth and viscous forces dominate. Boundary layer thickness grows slowly as $\delta \propto \sqrt{x}$.
2. **Transition Zone ($Re_{cr} \approx 5 \times 10^5$):** Small 2D Tollmien-Schlichting waves amplify into 3D turbulent spots.
3. **Turbulent Zone ($Re_x > 5 \times 10^5$):** Intense eddy mixing and momentum transfer occur. Thickness grows rapidly as $\delta \propto x^{0.8}$.
4. **Viscous Sublayer:** A sub-millimeter sublayer right at the solid surface where molecular viscosity dominates over eddy viscosity.

> [!tip] Practical Engineering Tip
> In ship model testing and wind tunnel flumes, boundary layer "trip wires" or sand strips are added near the leading edge to artificially trigger transition, ensuring representative turbulent scaling.

### Example 1.1: Transition Distance in a Laboratory Flume
**Problem:** Water at $20^\circ\text{C}$ ($ν = 1.00 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$) flows at $U_\infty = 2.0 \text{ m/s}$ past a smooth flat plate of length $L = 2.5 \text{ m}$. Assuming $Re_{cr} = 5 \times 10^5$:
1. Determine the distance from the leading edge where transition begins.
2. Find the Reynolds number at the trailing edge.

**Solution:**
1. **Transition Distance $x_{tr}$:**
   $$x_{tr} = \frac{Re_{cr} \cdot \nu}{U_\infty} = \frac{5 \times 10^5 \times 1.00 \times 10^{-6}}{2.0} = 0.25 \text{ m} = 250 \text{ mm}$$
2. **Trailing Edge Reynolds Number $Re_L$:**
   $$Re_L = \frac{U_\infty L}{\nu} = \frac{2.0 \times 2.5}{1.00 \times 10^{-6}} = 5.0 \times 10^6$$
   Since $Re_L > 5 \times 10^5$, 90% of the plate length operates under a fully turbulent boundary layer.

<!--tab:Summary-->
### Key Takeaways
- Boundary layer isolates viscous effects to a narrow shear region of thickness $\delta(x)$.
- Outside $\delta$, flow behaves as frictionless potential flow.
- Transition on smooth plates occurs at $Re_x = \frac{U_\infty x}{\nu} \approx 5 \times 10^5$.
