> [!note] Core Concept
> Accurate hydraulic simulation depends on the quality of the computational mesh. In traditional CFD, high resolution demands millions of microscopic cells. Modern software like **HEC-RAS 2D** revolutionizes this using **Subgrid Bathymetry**, extracting hydraulic properties from high-resolution LiDAR DEMs into pre-computed tables for larger computational cells.

## 1. Mesh Types and Mesh Quality Metrics
1. **Structured Grids:** Regular Cartesian or curvilinear quadrilaterals. Computationally efficient, but difficult to conform to meandering river bends.
2. **Unstructured Grids:** Arbitrary triangles, quadrilaterals, and general polygons (up to 8 faces per cell in HEC-RAS). Readily adapts to irregular shorelines, road embankments, and bridge piers.

Mesh Quality Diagnostics:
- **Aspect Ratio:** Ratio of longest cell dimension to shortest dimension (ideal $\approx 1.0$, acceptable $< 4.0$).
- **Orthogonality:** Angle between the face normal vector and the vector connecting adjacent cell centroids (ideal $90^\circ$).
- **Face Alignment with Flow:** Grid faces should align parallel or perpendicular to dominant streamlines to minimize **false numerical diffusion**.

## 2. Subgrid Bathymetry Technology
In classic CFD, each cell has a single flat elevation. If a levee is narrower than cell size $\Delta x$, the levee is missed, causing artificial leakage.
In **HEC-RAS Subgrid Bathymetry**:
- Computational cells can be $20 \text{ m} \times 20 \text{ m}$, but underlaid with a $1 \text{ m}$ LiDAR terrain DEM.
- For each cell, HEC-RAS pre-computes an **Elevation-Volume Curve** ($V(z)$).
- For each cell face, it pre-computes an **Elevation-Area Curve** ($A(z)$) and **Elevation-Wetted Perimeter Curve** ($P(z)$).
This enables coarse meshes to accurately represent channel bathymetry and levee overtopping without millions of tiny cells!

### Example 41.1: Elevation-Volume Table for a Subgrid Cell
**Problem:** A $10 \text{ m} \times 10 \text{ m}$ 2D computational cell (plan area $100 \text{ m}^2$) overlies an irregular triangular ditch terrain. The minimum bed elevation inside the cell is $z_0 = 100.00 \text{ m}$. As water level $z$ rises, the wetted surface area within the cell expands linearly as $A_{wet}(z) = 40 (z - 100.00) \text{ m}^2$ up to $z = 102.50 \text{ m}$, after which the full $100 \text{ m}^2$ is inundated.
Compute the water volume stored inside the cell at stage $z = 101.50 \text{ m}$.

**Solution:**
Water volume is the integral of wetted surface area with respect to elevation:
$$V(z) = \int_{z_0}^z A_{wet}(\zeta) \, d\zeta$$
For $z = 101.50 \text{ m}$ ($z \le 102.50 \text{ m}$):
$$V(101.50) = \int_{100.00}^{101.50} 40 (\zeta - 100.00) \, d\zeta$$
Let $\eta = \zeta - 100.00$:
$$V = 40 \int_0^{1.50} \eta \, d\eta = 40 \left[\frac{\eta^2}{2}\right]_0^{1.50} = 40 \times \frac{(1.50)^2}{2} = 40 \times 1.125 = 45.0 \text{ m}^3$$
At water surface stage $101.50 \text{ m}$, the cell stores precisely **$45.0 \text{ m}^3$** of water, capturing the true ditch shape inside a single 2D cell.

### Key Takeaways
- Unstructured meshes adapt to complex hydraulic geometries and breaklines.
- Subgrid bathymetry decouples computational cell size from topographic DEM resolution.
- Elevation-volume and elevation-conveyance tables provide sub-cell hydraulic precision.
