> [!note] Fundamental Core Definition
> Flow in an open channel or in a closed conduit having a free liquid surface is referred to as **free-surface flow** or **open-channel flow**. Open Channel Hydraulics deals with channels constructed for water supply, irrigation, drainage, sewers, culverts, tunnels flowing partially full, and natural streams and rivers.

## 1. Pipe Flow vs. Open Channel Flow: Comprehensive Comparison
The flow in a closed conduit is not necessarily pipe flow. If the liquid has a free surface, it must be classified as open channel flow:

| Parameter | Pipe Flow | Open Channel Flow |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cross-Sectional Confinement** | Flow completely fills the closed pipe conduit under internal pressure. Wetted perimeter $P = \pi D$ (100% boundary contact). | Liquid has a free surface exposed to atmospheric pressure. Top width $T$ is exposed to air; $P$ includes only bed and banks. |
| **Driving Mechanism** | Takes place due to a difference in piezometric pressure (pressure gradient: $\Delta P / L$). Can flow uphill under pump head! | Takes place due to the longitudinal slope of the channel bed under the action of **gravity** ($W \sin \theta$). Requires $S_0 > 0$. |
| **Hydraulic Gradient Line (HGL)** | Sum of elevation and pressure head ($z + P/\gamma$), connecting water levels in piezometers. Rises high above pipe crown. | Corresponds directly to the **Water Surface Line (WSL)**, where gauge pressure is zero ($P_{atm}$ reference). |
| **Energy Gradient Line (EGL)** | Sum of HGL and velocity head ($z + P/\gamma + V^2/2g$). Drops in flow direction by frictional head loss $h_L$. | Sum of WSL and velocity head ($z + y + V^2/2g$). Drops in flow direction by head loss $h_L$. |
| **Uniform Flow Gradient** | Drop in HGL equals the frictional head loss $h_L$. Conduit slope does not need to equal friction slope. | The total energy line, water surface line, and channel bottom are all strictly parallel: **$S_f = S_w = S_0 = S$**. Drop in EGL equals drop in bed. |

## 2. Classification of Open Channels: Origin, Geometry & Boundary Rigidity
Open channels can be classified systematically according to four foundational criteria:

1. **Based on Origin & Existence:**
   - **Natural Channels:** Streams, brooks, torrents, major rivers, estuaries, and tidal lagoons. These channels have inherently irregular geometries, shifting alignments, dynamic bed forms (pools and riffles), and boundary roughness that varies across seasons.
   - **Artificial Channels:** Man-made canals, flumes, aqueducts, sewers, drainage ditches, and laboratory flumes. Built with regular geometric cross-sections (trapezoidal, rectangular, triangular, circular) and constructed with known, predictable boundary materials (smooth concrete, ashlar masonry, corrugated metal).
2. **Based on Geometry along Length (Prismatic vs. Non-Prismatic):**
   - **Prismatic Channels:** A channel is defined as **prismatic** if its cross-sectional shape, dimensions, and bed slope $S_0$ remain strictly constant along the entire reach. Most engineered artificial channels are prismatic.
   - **Non-Prismatic Channels:** Channels in which cross-section, alignment, or bed slope vary along the direction of flow. All natural streams are non-prismatic; artificial channels can also be non-prismatic at transitions, chutes, and spillway contractions.
3. **Based on Boundary Rigidity (Rigid vs. Mobile Boundaries):**
   - **Rigid-Boundary Channels:** Channels lined with concrete, stone masonry, or steel where the boundary surface is non-erodible. The bed and banks cannot deform; the channel geometry is independent of the flow and has only **one degree of freedom** (the depth of flow $y$ varies with discharge $Q$).
   - **Mobile (Alluvial) Channels:** Channels excavated in loose sand, silt, or gravel where the moving water shapes its own boundary through erosion, sediment transport, deposition, and meandering. These channels possess **four degrees of freedom**: depth $y$, width $B$, bed slope $S_0$, and planform curvature/meander wavelength all adjust dynamically to reach equilibrium (regime theory).

## 3. Classification of Open Channel Flows: Time & Space Matrix
Flow in open channels is classified with respect to time $t$ and distance along the reach $x$:

- **Based on Time Criterion ($t$):**
  - **Steady Flow ($\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = 0, \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = 0$):** Depth, velocity, and discharge remain constant with respect to time at any given cross-section.
  - **Unsteady Flow ($\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} \ne 0, \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \ne 0$):** Depth, velocity, or discharge vary with time at a given cross-section (e.g. passage of a flood wave).
- **Based on Space Criterion ($x$):**
  - **Uniform Flow ($\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = 0, \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = 0$):** Depth and velocity remain identical from section to section along the channel reach. The water surface is parallel to the channel bed ($S_w = S_0$).
  - **Non-Uniform (Varied) Flow ($\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} \ne 0$):** Depth, velocity, or cross-sectional area change along the length of the channel.
    - **Gradually Varied Flow (GVF):** Depth changes smoothly and gradually over long channel distances (e.g. backwater curve upstream of a barrage or dam).
    - **Rapidly Varied Flow (RVF):** Depth changes abruptly over a very short distance, accompanied by intense shock waves and roller vortices (e.g. hydraulic jump, drop structure, free overfall).

## 4. Fundamental Forces & Governing Dimensionless Numbers
Fluid motion in open channels is governed by the equilibrium of six fundamental physical forces:

1. **Inertia Force ($F_i = \rho L^2 V^2$):** Mass times acceleration, representing the kinetic energy and momentum carried by the moving stream.
2. **Gravity Force ($F_g = \rho L^3 g$):** The primary driving force in open channels, acting through the downslope weight component $W \sin \theta$.
3. **Viscous Force ($F_\nu = \mu L V$):** Molecular shear resistance retarding fluid layers and generating boundary friction.
4. **Pressure Force ($F_p = \Delta P L^2$):** Hydrostatic thrust prisms acting across channel cross-sections.
5. **Surface Tension Force ($F_s = \sigma L$):** Molecular cohesion acting at the water-air interface.
6. **Elastic Force ($F_e = K L^2$):** Resistance of the fluid to volumetric compression.

### The Governing Dimensionless Parameters
By taking ratios of inertia force to each opposing force, fluid mechanics establishes five dimensionless numbers:

1. **The Froude Number ($Fr = \sqrt{\frac{F_i}{F_g}} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{g D}}$):**
   - The Froude number represents the ratio of flow velocity $V$ to the celerity of an elementary surface gravity wave $c = \sqrt{g D}$ (where $D = A/T$ is the hydraulic depth).
   - **Subcritical Flow ($Fr < 1$, Tranquil / Streaming):** Wave celerity exceeds flow velocity ($c > V$). Surface ripples can travel **upstream** against the current! Any downstream disturbance (such as a dam or gate) is communicated upstream. Therefore, **control is located downstream**.
   - **Critical Flow ($Fr = 1$):** Flow velocity equals wave celerity ($V = c$). Elementary gravity waves stand still as stationary waves. Specific energy is minimum for a given discharge.
   - **Supercritical Flow ($Fr > 1$, Shooting / Rapid):** Flow velocity exceeds wave celerity ($V > c$). Disturbances cannot propagate upstream; surface waves are swept downstream in a Mach-like wedge. Therefore, **control is located upstream**.
2. **The Reynolds Number ($Re = \frac{F_i}{F_\nu} = \frac{V R}{\nu}$):**
   - In open channels, the characteristic length is the **Hydraulic Radius $R = A/P$** (unlike pipes, where diameter $D = 4R$ is used).
   - **Laminar Flow:** $Re < 500$ (smooth parallel sheets; rare, found in thin sheet wash over hillslopes).
   - **Transition Flow:** $500 \le Re \le 2000$.
   - **Turbulent Flow:** $Re > 2000$ (intense eddy mixing; all civil engineering canals operate at $Re \sim 10^5 \text{ to } 10^7$).
3. **The Weber Number ($We = \sqrt{\frac{F_i}{F_s}} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{\sigma / (\rho L)}}$):**
   - Dictates surface tension effects. Critical in physical scale models of weirs, flumes, and spillways where shallow depths ($< 50 \text{ mm}$) can introduce capillary scale errors.

## 5. Geometric Parameters & Multi-Section Hydraulic Equivalence
For any arbitrary cross-section, the channel geometry is characterized by:
- **Depth ($y$):** Vertical distance from channel invert (lowest point of bed) to free surface.
- **Wetted Area ($A$):** Cross-sectional area occupied by water ($\text{m}^2$).
- **Wetted Perimeter ($P$):** Linear boundary length in contact with water ($\text{m}$).
- **Hydraulic Radius ($R = A/P$):** The primary indicator of hydraulic conveyance.
- **Top Width ($T$):** Surface width exposed to atmospheric air ($\text{m}$).
- **Hydraulic Depth ($D = A/T$):** Average water depth across the width.

### How Different Channels Can Share the Exact Same Hydraulic Radius ($R$)
By Manning's equation ($Q = \frac{1}{n} A R^{2/3} S_0^{1/2}$), the discharge velocity depends directly on $R$. A deep, narrow canal can have the **identical Hydraulic Radius $R$** as a wide, shallow river reach:
- In a wide river with $B \gg y$, wetted perimeter $P \approx B$, so $R = (B y)/B \approx y$.
- In a rectangular flume of width $B_2$, $R = \frac{B_2 y_2}{B_2 + 2y_2}$. Equating this to $R$, we can design multiple alternative channel sections that carry identical unit velocity under the same slope!

## 6. Cross-Section Velocity Contours (Isovels) & Velocity Dip
Because of boundary shear stresses along the wetted perimeter, velocity drops to zero at the channel bed and sidewalls (the no-slip boundary condition).

### Why the Maximum Velocity Dips Below the Free Surface
In open channels, the point of maximum velocity is **not at the free water surface**, but rather **$0.05 y \text{ to } 0.25 y$ below the surface**:
1. **Transverse Secondary Circulation Cells:** Friction at the corner sidewalls generates inward secondary vortex loops along the free surface that converge toward the centerline, plunging slow fluid downward and suppressing peak velocity beneath the surface.
2. **Surface Air Resistance:** Minor shear between the flowing water surface and the stagnant ambient atmospheric air also retards surface particles.
3. **Aspect Ratio Influence:** In narrow flumes ($B/y < 5$), corner sidewall friction is strong, producing a pronounced velocity dip ($0.20 y - 0.25 y$). In wide rivers ($B/y > 10$), sidewall effects are negligible across the central reach, and max velocity occurs virtually at the surface.

### Field Hydrometry Standards (IS: 1192 & USGS)
Because point velocity varies continuously across depth, field engineers take measurements with current meters at calibrated fractions of depth:
- **Shallow Streams ($y < 0.6 \text{ m}$):** One-point method at $0.6 y$ depth from the surface:
  $$V_{\text{mean}} \approx v_{0.6}$$
- **Deep Channels ($y \ge 0.6 \text{ m}$):** Two-point average at $0.2 y$ and $0.8 y$ depth:
  $$V_{\text{mean}} \approx \frac{v_{0.2} + v_{0.8}}{2}$$
Both empirical standards closely match the rigorous analytical depth-integral of the non-uniform profile.

### Example 10.1: Classification of Flow Regime (Re and Fr)
**Problem:** A rectangular concrete canal ($B = 4.0 \text{ m}$) carries water at depth $y = 1.2 \text{ m}$ at a discharge of $Q = 14.4 \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$. Water kinematic viscosity is $\nu = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$.
1. Compute the hydraulic radius $R$ and hydraulic depth $D$.
2. Calculate the Reynolds number $Re$ and determine if the flow is laminar or turbulent.
3. Calculate the Froude number $Fr$ and determine whether the flow is subcritical (tranquil/streaming) or supercritical (shooting/rapid).

:::sol Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
1. **Geometric Elements:**
   - Flow Area: $A = B y = 4.0 \times 1.2 = 4.80 \text{ m}^2$
   - Wetted Perimeter: $P = B + 2y = 4.0 + 2(1.2) = 6.40 \text{ m}$
   - Hydraulic Radius: $R = \frac{A}{P} = \frac{4.80}{6.40} = \mathbf{0.750 \text{ m}}$
   - Top Width: $T = B = 4.0 \text{ m}$
   - Hydraulic Depth: $D = \frac{A}{T} = \frac{4.80}{4.0} = \mathbf{1.20 \text{ m}}$

2. **Reynolds Number:**
   - Velocity: $V = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{14.4}{4.80} = 3.0 \text{ m/s}$
   - $Re = \frac{V R}{\nu} = \frac{3.0 \times 0.750}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 2.25 \times 10^6$
   - *Regime:* Since $Re = 2.25 \times 10^6 \gg 2000$, flow is **fully turbulent**.

3. **Froude Number:**
   - Wave Celerity: $c = \sqrt{g D} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 1.20} = \sqrt{11.772} = 3.431 \text{ m/s}$
   - Froude Number: $Fr = \frac{V}{c} = \frac{3.0}{3.431} = \mathbf{0.874}$
   - *Regime:* Since $Fr = 0.874 < 1.0$, the flow is **Sub-Critical (Tranquil / Streaming)**. Small surface ripples can travel upstream against the flow.
:::

### Example 10.2: Energy & Momentum Correction Factors
**Problem:** In a wide rectangular channel of depth $y_0 = 1.8 \text{ m}$, field gauging determines that the velocity profile follows the power law $u(y) = 2.4 \left(\frac{y}{y_0}\right)^{1/7} \text{ m/s}$.
Calculate:
1. Mean velocity $V$.
2. Kinetic energy correction coefficient $\alpha$ (Coriolis coefficient).
3. Momentum correction coefficient $\beta$ (Boussinesq coefficient).

:::sol Detailed Analytical Derivation
1. **Mean Velocity $V$:**
   $$V = \frac{1}{y_0}\int_0^{y_0} 2.4 \left(\frac{y}{y_0}\right)^{1/7} dy = \frac{2.4}{y_0} \left[\frac{y^{8/7}}{8/7}\right]_0^{y_0} = 2.4 \times \frac{7}{8} = \mathbf{2.10 \text{ m/s}}$$

2. **Momentum Correction Factor $\beta$:**
   $$\beta = \frac{1}{A V^2} \int u^2 dA = \frac{1}{y_0 V^2} \int_0^{y_0} (2.4)^2 \left(\frac{y}{y_0}\right)^{2/7} dy = \frac{5.76}{y_0 (2.10)^2} \left[\frac{y^{9/7}}{9/7}\right]_0^{y_0} = \frac{5.76}{4.41} \times \frac{7}{9} = \frac{40.32}{39.69} = \mathbf{1.016}$$

3. **Kinetic Energy Correction Factor $\alpha$:**
   $$\alpha = \frac{1}{A V^3} \int u^3 dA = \frac{1}{y_0 V^3} \int_0^{y_0} (2.4)^3 \left(\frac{y}{y_0}\right)^{3/7} dy = \frac{13.824}{y_0 (2.10)^3} \left[\frac{y^{10/7}}{10/7}\right]_0^{y_0} = \frac{13.824}{9.261} \times \frac{7}{10} = \mathbf{1.045}$$
   *Verification:* Notice that $\alpha (1.045) > \beta (1.016) > 1.00$, confirming Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
:::

### Real-World Field Nuances & Standards
1. **Partially Full Tunnels and Sewers (IS: 4111):**
   Sewers and road drainage tunnels are closed circular conduits, but they are specifically designed to flow **partially full** under gravity ($y/D \approx 0.75 - 0.80$). This ensures an open free surface for sewer ventilation, preventing explosive buildup of hydrogen sulfide ($H_2S$) and methane gases.
2. **Velocity Dip Measurement Standards (IS: 1192 / USGS):**
   In field hydrometry, when measuring mean stream velocity with a current meter:
   - For shallow channels ($y < 0.6 \text{ m}$): Take reading at **$0.6 y$ depth** from the surface ($V_{mean} \approx V_{0.6}$).
   - For deep channels ($y \ge 0.6 \text{ m}$): Take the two-point average at **$0.2 y$ and $0.8 y$ depth**:
     $$V_{mean} = \frac{V_{0.2} + V_{0.8}}{2}$$
   This standard accounts for boundary shear and the surface velocity dip.
